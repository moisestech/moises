We inherit:

cities that are quietly reprogrammed by logistics apps and real-estate models,

a culture where intelligence can be purchased by the token.

We learn to call this “normal.”

For a long time, I treated that normal as a given: something I could critique in my art, but ultimately had to accept. Scroll, make work, apply for shows, learn new software; repeat. It felt like surfing: as long as I stayed upright, I could ride the waves of whatever new tools appeared.

Then I had my first real AI k-hole.

It happened with Suno, an AI music generator. The first evening was pure joy. I typed in a prompt and heard a fully produced track in a style I’d never had the musical training to execute. Then another. Then another. In a couple of days I had made more “songs” than in years. Genres I had only admired from afar were suddenly at my fingertips. I felt like I had found a cheat code to my own imagination.

A few days later, the high warped into something else.

I caught myself looping prompts late into the night, chasing slightly better variations, listening to tracks I couldn’t even fully process before generating new ones. It felt good and a little sick—like eating dessert past the point of hunger. I asked my wife if it sounded like addiction. I ran a poll online. Most people said no; they didn’t feel that way about it. But my body did. Something in me had crossed a line.

In that moment, AI stopped being just a collaborator I could invite in or out of the process. It started to feel like an atmosphere—a surrounding condition where the easiest, most frictionless choice was always “do one more.” One more track. One more variation. One more prompt.

It was the same sensation I’d had years earlier with social media, but amplified and inverted. Social feeds addict you through consumption. This new wave of tools addicts you through production. It’s not just doomscrolling; it’s doom-creating. You don’t just watch the feed—you help fill it.

That’s when the phrase “born into the machine” clicked for me in a new way:

The machine isn’t just the hardware and software out there.
It’s also the invisible logic that starts colonizing your sense of what’s possible, what counts, and how fast you should be moving.

I’m writing this book because I don’t think that feeling is going to stay niche. Right now, only a few of us push these tools hard enough to hit the k-hole; we’re the early divers. But as AI seeps into everything—from work to school to prayer to parenting—that line between “use” and “environment” is going to blur for everyone.

The question isn’t: “Will we live in the machine?”
We already do.

The real questions are:

How do we learn to see the machine that we’re already born into?

How do we negotiate with it, instead of drifting along on default settings?

What role can art, rituals, and small practices play in making those negotiations visible and feelable?

3. Naming the Climate: Fisher, Han, Berardi

Before I ever had language for this feeling—the workstation as weather, the AI k-hole, the treadmill as a lifestyle—I had the symptoms.

I knew what it felt like to sit down and feel behind before I even opened a single tab. I knew what it felt like to doomscroll until my eyes hurt, not out of joy but out of inertia. And I knew what it felt like to be more afraid of “falling behind” than of being exhausted.

Later, I started finding people who had been describing this climate long before we called it “AI.”

Mark Fisher: the atmosphere you can’t step outside

Mark Fisher called it capitalist realism: the sense that it’s easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of the current system. It’s not just an idea; it’s an atmosphere. You breathe it without realizing. It tells you, quietly but relentlessly, that whatever exists now is the only serious option. You can change outfits, apps, hobbies—but not the underlying logic.

When I think of Fisher, I see myself on the Doomscrolling Treadmill.

Physically, I’m walking in place. The belt moves under me; the content moves past me. I scroll through TikTok, news, finance, AI demos, memes. The treadmill goes nowhere, but my nervous system is traveling at 200 kilometers an hour. That’s Fisher’s atmosphere made literal: a body moving and not moving at the same time, inside a system that pretends infinite motion is the same as progress.

He also talked about “depressive hedonia”—not classic sadness, but a restless, low-level compulsion to keep consuming pleasures that don’t nourish you. That phrase sits right next to my Suno episode. I wasn’t sad while making those hundreds of tracks. I was… compelled. Rewarded. Slightly hollowed out afterward. That’s depressive hedonia in the age of AI utilities: not just Netflix next episode, but “regenerate one more time.”

Fisher helps me say:
this isn’t just my weird habit. It’s a pattern the system produces in bodies like mine.

Byung-Chul Han: the achievement subject at the workstation

If Fisher describes the air, Byung-Chul Han describes the posture.

In The Burnout Society, he says we’ve shifted from being obedience subjects (“do this because I told you”) to achievement subjects (“I can do anything if I try harder”). We tell ourselves we’re free, entrepreneurial, creative—even as we slowly burn out from self-exploitation. There’s no need for a boss to yell at us; our own aspirations crack the whip.

My workstation is an altar to the achievement subject.

On the table: laptop, notebook, external monitor, phone, maybe a second machine running some training job. In my head: an endless list—finish the email, refactor the code, write the grant, help the students, post about the show, archive the performance, learn the new model, answer the DMs, be a good partner, call your family, drink water, stretch.

Han talks about how we internalize productivity as moral worth. I feel that whenever I look at the desk and think: if I’m not working here, I’m wasting time. Even my experiments in “self-care” risk becoming optimization projects: sleep eight hours so I can output more; exercise so I can sit longer; meditate so my focus improves.

Doomscrolling Treadmill was partly my way of making this ridiculousness visible. If I’m going to answer emails, doomscroll, and try to stay physically healthy at the same time, why not literally put them on the same machine? Why not externalize this multi-tasking achievement subject as a performance: body walking, brain scrolling, laptop glowing, TikTok chirping, all humming along in one tight loop?

Han helps me admit that burnout isn’t a personal failure here. It’s a logical consequence of a design where everything—work, rest, joy, relationships—is quietly repackaged as potential productivity.

Franco “Bifo” Berardi: velocity without a future

If Fisher gives us the air and Han gives us the posture, Franco “Bifo” Berardi gives us the timeline.

In After the Future, he argues that the 20th century lived on a huge collective story called “The Future”: progress, growth, rockets, revolutions, better tomorrows. That story has collapsed. The future didn’t arrive the way it was promised. Instead, we got deregulation, financial crises, climate breakdown, and an internet that feels more like a casino than a library.

What’s left, Berardi says, is a strange state where acceleration continues, but direction disappears. We still move faster—more data, more notifications, more upgrades—but it’s not clear where any of it is going. That produces a mix of panic and exhaustion he calls “semiocapitalism”: a regime where language, images, and signs are the main raw materials.

That feels very close to what I mean by “born into the machine.”

My days are full of velocity: new models, patches, frameworks, deadlines. Miami is full of velocity: condos appearing overnight, crypto conferences, AI festivals, delivery robots, hurricanes crossing the map. The part that feels broken isn’t speed itself; it’s horizon. Faster than what, and towards where?

Berardi helps me see that my obsession with treadmill pieces, doomscrolling, and “dead internet cities” isn’t accidental. They’re attempts to stage velocity without horizon. To show in concrete, bodily terms what it means to be running inside a system that keeps hitting the gas even as its story of the future falls apart.

Tool → Climate

Together, Fisher, Han, and Berardi sketch the larger shape of the environment I’m trying to make tangible:

Fisher: the atmosphere that says, “This is the only serious game in town.”

Han: the subject position that says, “You can do more; if you can’t, it’s your fault.”

Berardi: the time condition that says, “We’re going faster and faster, but don’t ask where to.”

AI slots straight into this shape.

It arrives marketed as a tool:
“Here, this will save you time. Here, this will make you more creative. Here, this will help you keep up.”

But once you plug enough of your life into it—your writing, your music, your teaching, your admin, your studio planning—it flips from tool to climate. It becomes part of the air, the posture, the timeline:

It thickens Fisher’s atmosphere: now it’s not just capitalism that feels inevitable, but also AI-mediated everything.

It pushes Han’s achievement subject: now you’re not just competing with other humans, but with synthetic productivity.

It cranks Berardi’s velocity: now iteration cycles that used to take weeks collapse into minutes.

I don’t want this book to stay at the level of theory, though. I’m not a philosopher in the abstract; I’m a working artist trying to build pieces, tools, and teaching practices inside this pressure system.

So I’m using these thinkers less as authorities and more as translation devices—ways of naming what my body has already learned from spending too many hours at the workstation, on the treadmill, and inside the generative k-hole.

They give me words.
The work gives me scenes.

This book is where those two meet.

4. What This Book Will Do

If the workstation, the doomscroll, and the AI k-hole are where this book starts, the goal isn’t to stay stuck there—staring at the problem, admiring the horror.

I’m not writing a doom report, and I’m not writing a hype manual.

I’m writing from the inside of a situation:
a human body, in a studio in Miami, trying to make art and infrastructure while the tools around me quietly turn into the climate I live in.

So this book will try to do three things at the same time:

Name the machine we’re already inside.

Show how it feels in the body, in the studio, and in the city.

Offer small, concrete ways to renegotiate that relationship.

I don’t believe in a clean “outside” anymore. There’s no pre-digital jungle we’re going back to; even the jungle is wired now. But I also don’t believe in letting the default settings run everything.

Somewhere between burnout and surrender, there’s a more interesting space:
a practice of living with the machine while refusing to let it quietly define what counts as value, meaning, or faith.

That’s the space this book wants to map.

From Dead Internet to Live Body

In the next chapter, I’ll stay close to the body.

We’ll sit with the treadmill, the workstation, the late-night phone glow. We’ll look at doomscrolling not as a moral failure but as a symptom of the climate Fisher, Han, and Berardi described long before TikTok, Suno, or GPT.

I’ll use my own pieces—Doomscrolling Treadmill, Doomscrolling Marathon, the RGB meditation rigs, even older works like Eye Plug and Laptop Face—as case studies in how the body contorts itself to fit the demands of a 24/7 machine that wants more attention, more content, more optimization.

The question there is simple and uncomfortable:

What does “dead internet” actually mean when you’re still alive and scrolling?

When Intelligence Becomes a Utility

From there, we zoom in on intelligence itself.

We’ll talk about the moment thinking became something you can rent by the token:
using ChatGPT to draft, Suno to compose, diffusion models to imagine, multi-agent systems to solve.

I’ll go back to that Suno binge and pull it apart:

how it felt like liberation,

how it tipped into dependency,

how that arc mirrors the broader shift from “AI as futuristic magic” to “AI as everyday infrastructure.”

Hinton, Wark, and Zuboff will come along for that ride—not to scare you with probabilities of extinction, but to make it clear that this utility has owners, and those owners have interests. Intelligence, at scale, is never neutral.

The question there:

If intelligence is now a service, how do we avoid becoming just another tier on someone else’s pricing page?

Slop, Cities, and the Stack

The middle of the book widens the frame.

We’ll walk through what I’m calling “dead internet cities”—places where surface life is saturated with synthetic media, automated logistics, and speculative architecture that looks like an AI render come to life.

Using Kate Crawford’s “AI slop” and Benjamin Bratton’s “Stack,” we’ll look at:

how generative slop isn’t just ugly content but a metabolic loop of extraction and waste,

how cities like Miami are already being quietly reprogrammed by these logics,

how my works—like Smart Shoppers, the e-waste floor, and treadmill pieces—fit into that metabolism.

We’ll also talk about Vectoral Miami specifically: the way real estate, crypto, tourism, and culture stack on top of each other; the way race and class are mirrored and intensified by “neutral” systems; the way an artist can still hijack a tiny slice of that stack to do something unexpected.

The questions here:

What does it mean to walk through a city that’s half cloud, half concrete?
How do you make art that lives in that overlap without becoming pure decoration for the vectoralist class?

Techno-Spiritual Futures

In the later chapters, the book moves into the space where tech and belief start mixing.

We’ll step into Simulation Faith and the imagined AI church entrances—not as jokes, but as serious questions:

What happens when recommendation engines start shaping not just what you watch, but what you worship?

How do small storefront churches, WhatsApp prayer chains, and Latin American devotional cultures adapt to or resist AI-mediated faith?

Can an AI pastor, a prayer bot, or a generative saint ever be more than another UX surface for a data-extracting stack?

Erik Davis, Yuk Hui, and Federico Campagna will be friends here, helping us think:

about mysticism inside machines,

about cosmotechnics (different ways of tying cosmos + tech),

about the difference between Technic (pure instrumentality) and Magic (meaning that can’t be reduced to a function).

The questions:

Are we building new temples or just new interfaces?
Where, if anywhere, does the sacred live when everything can be rendered, captioned, and optimized?

Children of the Utility & Small Rituals

Finally, we’ll look ahead—not in terms of sci-fi spectacle, but in terms of everyday futures:

kids raised with AI copilots,

UBI as potential buffer or trap,

AI-mediated schooling and care.

We’ll talk about how a world of “intelligence as a utility” splits childhood into classes:

those who can still afford human time,

those who get automated attention,

those who are mostly data sources feeding someone else’s model.

Then we’ll land on what I’m calling small rituals in a fast machine:

micro-practices, studio habits, workshop formats, and tool choices that don’t solve the whole system—but do carve out pockets of autonomy, clarity, and shared meaning.

Things like:

using AI to slow down and think through a problem, not just to produce more,

building artworks that expose the pipes, not just the output,

teaching “Home Ec for AI”—basic literacy that treats prompting, data, and models as household infrastructure you should understand, not magic you obey.

The last question is the one I care about most:

If we are born into the machine, how do we avoid living like we’re already dead inside it?

I don’t think there’s a single, clean answer. But I do think there are better and worse ways of inhabiting this moment—more honest stories we can tell, more grounded practices we can share, and more precise images we can build together.

This book is my attempt to offer some of mine.
