1. The State and Revolution – Vladimir Lenin

What it is
Written in 1917, just before the October Revolution, Lenin lays out what the state is (for him: an instrument of class domination), why a proletarian revolution is necessary, and what “dictatorship of the proletariat” and the “withering away of the state” mean. 
Wikipedia
+1

Who Lenin is
Revolutionary leader of the Bolsheviks, architect of the Soviet state, and one of the core figures of 20th-century Marxism. This is often considered his most important theoretical work on the state. 
Wikipedia

How to read it

Think of it as the classical base layer for everything else in the stack.

Read slowly and maybe alongside a good summary; it’s polemical but not that long.

As you read, mentally connect his idea of the state with platform power today (platforms as new “organs of force”).

6. Of Grammatology – Jacques Derrida

What it is
A dense 1967 philosophical work that basically launches deconstruction. Derrida argues Western thought has privileged speech over writing; he unravels the idea of pure “presence” and shows how meaning is always deferred, mediated by signs. 
Wikipedia
+1

Who Derrida is
French philosopher associated with post-structuralism. Massive influence across literature, art theory, and critical theory. 
Wikipedia

How to read it

This is hard mode. You don’t need to read cover-to-cover right away.

Use a guide or secondary commentary; focus on the basic intuitions: no pure origin, everything is mediated, context matters. Perfect for thinking about AI training data, prompts, and remix culture.

7. Industrial Society and Its Future – Ted Kaczynski (Unabomber Manifesto)

What it is
A 35,000-word 1995 anti-technology manifesto arguing that industrial/technological society destroys freedom, nature, and psychological well-being, and that technological progress is inherently uncontrollable.

Who Kaczynski is
Mathematician turned domestic terrorist (“Unabomber”) who carried out a bombing campaign from 1978–1995; three people were killed, many injured. The manifesto was published after he threatened further violence.

4. How to keep an “objective” relationship to the wild texts

A simple practice as you read:

Name the position before judging it.

e.g. “This is accelerationist right-wing nihilism,” or “This is anti-tech primitivism,” or “This is left media-theory pessimism.”

Ask two questions for each author:

What do they see very clearly that others ignore?

What do they systematically refuse to see?

Map your distance in a sentence.

“I share Fisher’s sense of capitalist realism, but I don’t want to abandon spirituality.”

“Land is useful for feeling the pull of acceleration but politically I’m absolutely elsewhere.”

Over time you’ll have your own inner compass: a small canon of 5–7 people who feel like “my extended thought-family,” and a set of others who are important neighbors or antagonists.
