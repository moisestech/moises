Chapter 2 – Dead Internet, Live Body
1. Scrolling in Place

The first time I did the Doomscrolling Treadmill as a performance, I remember how ordinary it felt at the beginning.

There’s a treadmill, a TV, a laptop, a phone. Nothing about that is exotic. People walk on treadmills and stare at screens every day—in gyms, in garages, in apartments where the running machine doubles as a clothing rack. I wasn’t inventing anything new. I was just deciding to do it on purpose.

The premise was simple:
I’d walk and scroll and work for a long stretch of time, surrounded by screens. TikTok on one, code or email on another, maybe a book nearby that I’d pretend to consult. No meditation music, no nature shots, no wellness slogans. Just me, my body slowly tiring out, and an endless vertical feed of video fragments that the algorithm thought I should see.

From the outside, the scene reads as critique:
“Look at this poor guy, literally walking in place while he doomscrolls, trapped by technology, etc.”

From the inside, the feeling is much more confusing.

At first, it’s productive. I’m answering messages, checking links, catching up on news, watching tutorials, clipping references. I’m moving, my heart rate is up, I feel less guilty about being “online” because at least my legs are doing something. It feels like a life hack: if scrolling is inevitable, why not turn it into cardio?

After a while, the productive layer thins out. I’m not really remembering what I’m seeing. I’m not finishing videos, I’m just sampling them. The phone starts to feel heavy in my hand, but I don’t put it down. The treadmill hums. The feed refreshes. I scroll not because I want to, but because my thumb already knows how.

The piece is called Doomscrolling Treadmill, but if I’m honest, it could just be called Thursday.

That’s the part I’m trying to catch in this chapter:
the way the “dead internet” isn’t just a network problem or a cultural theory, but a set of gestures you can watch in real time:

a thumb flicking upward,

a neck tilting down,

a back curling toward the screen,

a body moving in place while the world’s information rushes past it.

The phrase “dead internet” gets used online to describe something paranoid and grand: the idea that a lot of what we’re seeing now is bots talking to bots, fake users, recycled content, AI-generated slop stitched together to look like life. And there’s some truth to that. But at the level of the body, “dead internet” feels different.

It feels like:

logging on out of habit and leaving more drained than when you arrived,

seeing the same joke in ten formats and laughing at none of them,

watching content that looks ultra-alive—hyper-saturated, hyper-edited, hyper-optimized—and feeling less alive inside your own skin.

It feels like walking and not going anywhere.

That’s why the treadmill was the right machine for this project. It’s honest. It doesn’t lie about the loop. If you watch someone sprint on a treadmill for half an hour and then step off in the exact same place, you don’t call that “travel.” You call it “exercise.” You call it “keeping up.”

When I mount a treadmill in a white cube or a festival space and plug TikTok into it, I’m not trying to say, “Screens bad, outside good.” I’m trying to show what we already know but rarely stage as theater:

The internet might be dead in some abstract way,
but our bodies are still very alive, and we’re spending that aliveness inside it.

The tired legs are real. The dry eyes are real. The nervous system ping-ponging between outrage, arousal, boredom, and envy is real. Whatever the bots are doing behind the scenes, our muscles are the ones holding the phone.

In my own life, the treadmill is just one device among many. Most days, the “treadmill” is a chair and a desk.

I sit at my workstation and feel a similar kind of motionless movement:

inbox zero as cardio,

task lists as distance goals,

context switching as HIIT training.

I click through tabs the way I swipe through videos. There’s a constant feeling of catching up, even if I spent the whole day “getting things done.” The movement is internal: thoughts racing, pulse rising, dopamine loops firing. From the outside, I’m just sitting. From the inside, I’m sprinting.

So when I talk about a “dead internet” and a “live body,” I’m not making a clean opposition—like the internet is rotten and the flesh is pure. I’m describing a mismatch:

a nervous system evolved for scarcity, slowness, and local communities,

plugged into systems designed for abundance, speed, and global scale.

The doomscroll is what happens when that mismatch gets routinized. It becomes a nightly ritual. You lie down or sit, open the feed, and let the machine pump an unpredictable mixture of horror, humor, sexuality, product recommendations, and pseudo-therapy into your brain until it feels numb enough to sleep.

The treadmill performance just removes the bed and replaces it with a machine that doesn’t pretend to be rest.

When I look back at documentation photos from Doomscrolling Treadmill or Doomscrolling Marathon, I’m struck by how unremarkable they look. A guy, some screens, some tech. It only looks like “performance art” because it’s isolated, framed, lit, time-boxed. Put the same setup in a living room and suddenly it’s just “a person catching up online.”

That border between performance and daily life is part of what this chapter is about.

At what point does our use of these systems become so routinized that we no longer notice the cost to the body?

How many hours of walking, sitting, staring, and scrolling does it take before something in us starts to feel… flat?

And how do we represent that flatness without flattening ourselves even further?

This is where the idea of the “dead internet” intersects with the idea of the “achievement subject” from Chapter 1. It’s not just that the content is low quality or synthetic. It’s that we approach the internet as another arena where we’re supposed to perform: to stay informed, stay relevant, stay responsive, stay productive.

Logging off starts to feel like failure.
Ignoring a message feels impolite, unprofessional, or unsafe.
Not knowing about the latest crisis feels irresponsible.

So we keep the treadmill on.
We keep the feed running.
We keep moving in place.

In this chapter, I want to stay close to those movements. Before we climb back into theory, I want to trace the small, physical, almost boring gestures that add up to “life online”:

the way your hand automatically reaches for the phone when a task gets hard,

the way your eyes blur after switching between five different apps in two minutes,

the way you can feel utterly tired from “doing nothing.”

Because if we can’t describe the body inside the machine, all the big words about “dead internet,” “AI slop,” and “platform capitalism” risk floating away from lived reality. They become another kind of content we skim at high speed.

So let’s slow it down for a moment.

Let’s stay with the treadmill, the desk, the phone in the hand, the laptop glow on your face at 1:30 a.m.

Let’s look at how a live body behaves inside what increasingly feels like a dead or undead network—a system that keeps moving, but doesn’t necessarily move us anywhere worth going.

Only after that will we zoom back out to talk about slop, stacks, and smart cities.

For now, it’s just one body, one machine, one feed.

And the simple, unsettling question behind all of it:

If this is what “being connected” looks like in the flesh,
what exactly are we connected to?

2. From Doomscrolling to Doom-Creating

If doomscrolling is what happens when you let the internet pour itself into you, doom-creating is what happens when you pour yourself into the internet with machine help.

The first time I went deep with Suno, it felt like a jailbreak.

I’ve loved music my whole life, but I’m not a trained producer. I can hear arrangements in my head that my fingers can’t play. There are genres—reggaetón, electro-cumbia, weird industrial trap hybrids—that I adore as a listener but never had the technical scaffolding to inhabit as a maker.

Suno short-circuited that distance.

I typed in a prompt—something loose, like a mood and a rough style—and suddenly I was hearing a fully produced track: drums, bass, mixing, melodic hooks, all there. Not perfect, but coherent. Then another track. And another. Different genres, different moods. Within a couple of days, I had spawned hundreds of songs I never could have produced alone.

On paper, that’s a miracle.
In my body, it felt like a drug.

At first the high was: “I’m finally free.”

Free from gatekeeping: no need to find a producer, rent a studio, or justify the expense.
Free from training: no years of DAW tutorials, no nights wrestling with EQ.
Free from self-contempt: no gap between the imagined song and a garbage demo that makes you want to quit.

The model gave me what capitalism almost never gives artists: instant scale.

I could take a concept like “Holy Wi-Fi – Vice Ciudad” and spin it into multiple versions, like a multiverse of tracks, in an afternoon. I could experiment recklessly because each failure cost almost nothing. The bottleneck moved from technical capacity to imagination and curation.

That was the honeymoon phase.

Then something in my nervous system flipped.

I found myself generating new tracks before I had even listened all the way through the previous ones. I’d skim them the way I skim social videos: a verse here, a hook there, next. I wasn’t really finishing songs; I was sampling my own output. The act of creating became another form of scrolling.

What had started as liberation slid into compulsion.

A familiar logic crept in:

That last one was good, but what if the next one hits harder?

What if I tweak the prompt just a little?

What if I ask it for a different key, a different tempo, a different language?

This is the same intermittent reward structure that makes slot machines and feeds addictive, but turned toward production instead of consumption. The pull isn’t “one more episode” but “one more generation.”

I remember turning to my wife and saying, half joking, half serious:
“I think I could get addicted to this.”

I even ran a poll to test my own paranoia. Most people who’d tried generative music tools said they didn’t feel that way; they were curious, amused, maybe impressed, but not compelled. For them, the tool was still a plugin—an effect they could turn on and off.

For me, even that tiny piece of data was instructive.

It showed me something about my own wiring, sure. I like pushing systems until they bend. But it also showed me where the future average might be. The fact that I felt this dependency early doesn’t mean it’s rare. It just means I’m an early diver in a pool that’s still filling up.

What happens when tools like this are built into every platform, every device, every workflow? When the distance between idea and fully rendered content gets shaved down to seconds for everyone, all the time?

At that point, “addiction” starts to sound less like an edge case and more like a design feature.

The more I sat with that Suno period, the more I realized it was a cousin to the doomscroll treadmill:

Doomscrolling: the machine floods you with more content than you can process.

Doom-creating: you, with the machine, flood the world with more content than you can process.

In both cases, the result is the same:

your attention gets fragmented,

your sense of time blurs,

you end the session more tired than you began, without a clear memory of what just happened.

In both cases, the metrics look great:

minutes of “engagement,”

number of tracks created,

outputs per hour.

In both cases, the body pays.

The big difference is that doom-creating wraps the experience in ego and achievement. It feels virtuous:

I’m not just scrolling—I’m making.

I’m not just consuming slop—I’m generating my own.

This is creativity, not addiction.

That’s why it took me longer to recognize the same pattern.

If you tell someone you spend six hours a night doomscrolling on TikTok, most people will agree it sounds unhealthy. If you tell them you spent six hours a night “making music” or “writing” or “training” or “practicing prompts,” it sounds like discipline. Hustle. Passion.

But neurologically, the loop can be identical:
reward, boredom, reward, boredom; next, next, next.

The “dead internet” isn’t just dead on the consumption side. It’s dead on the production side too. We can now create at such velocity, and in such volume, that the individual work becomes almost meaningless to us even as we’re making it. It’s content as exhaust.

I’m not saying the tracks I made with Suno are worthless. Some of them still move me. Some of them will probably surface in future performances, videos, installations. But the experience of making them revealed something important:

There’s a threshold where the very tools that expand your creative capacity
also start to erode your ability to feel that creativity as something grounded, finite, and embodied.

When intelligence becomes a utility you can open like a tap, the question stops being “can I make this?” and becomes “why would I stop?” There is always one more variation available, one more genre to try, one more language to switch into.

This is where the language from Chapter 1 comes back in:

Fisher’s depressive hedonia: the restless chasing of stimulation without satisfaction.

Han’s achievement subject: the self that measures its worth in output, not in presence.

Berardi’s velocity without horizon: creative acceleration with no clear sense of destination.

The Suno binge was my private little lab where all three converged.

It also pointed to something crucial for the rest of this book:

The “dead internet” is not just a space we look at; it’s a logic we internalize.
It’s not just infinite scroll; it’s infinite generate.

And that has consequences.

It changes:

how we relate to our own skills (if the model can do it, what is “mine” exactly?),

how we relate to time (if everything can be made quickly, what deserves slowness?),

how we relate to others (if everyone can flood the world with their outputs, who is supposed to listen?).

It also changes what burnout looks like.

Old burnout:
too many tasks, not enough time, clear sense of limits.

New burnout:
apparently infinite creative capacity, muddy sense of purpose, background guilt for not doing more with the tools you have.

You don’t hit a wall because the tool runs out.
You hit a wall because you do.

For me, recognizing the Suno k-hole as a form of doom-creating was a small reclaiming of sanity. It gave me permission to put the laptop down, to treat the model not as a magical collaborator but as part of an environment I needed boundaries with.

That’s not a dramatic solution. It’s not a manifesto. It’s just a small act of naming:

This is a climate, not a neutral plugin.

My body is not designed to live in permanent generative flow.

I need intentional exits.

In the rest of this chapter, I want to stay with that tension:

how the body responds to systems that promise infinite creation,

how the nervous system tries to cope with dead-internet loops,

and how artworks—like Doomscrolling Treadmill, Doomscrolling Marathon, and future pieces around AI music—can function as mirrors, not answers.

Only after we’ve mapped that terrain will we zoom out, in later chapters, to talk about slop, cities, AI as infrastructure, and techno-spiritual futures.

Because before we can ask what the machine is doing to society, we have to understand what it’s already doing to our own hands, lungs, and hearts.

Chapter 3 – The Rooster and the Dinosaur
1. From Dinosaurs to Roosters: A Parable of Power

It’s a Miami morning and there’s a rooster on the sidewalk. It’s not remarkable, except in that sudden flash of perspective: this small creature is a distant descendant of the dinosaurs that once ruled the earth.

What happened between the reign of those massive creatures and the city-strolling rooster is a story of collapse and adaptation. The mightiest species can dwindle into something humbler, something that survives in the margins of a new world. And that’s a parable we can borrow when we think about human dominance in the age of AI and automation.

In this chapter, we’re pivoting from the intimate experience of doomscrolling and doom-creating to a wider lens: what happens to a dominant species when the world’s rules change? What if, in a century, the “apex” of human power looks as out of place as a dinosaur in a world of roosters?

2. Capitalism’s Evolutionary Game

This isn’t just about AI as a technology. It’s about the economic and cultural logic that rides alongside it. Capitalism is an evolutionary force. It’s not just an economic system; it’s a set of incentives that shape behavior, distribution of resources, and definitions of success. In a world where AI is owned and directed by a tiny fraction of humanity, the rest of us might indeed become something like those roosters: still here, still alive, but living in a world whose main rhythms and rules are set by forces we no longer control.

In this section, we’ll explore that analogy more deeply. We’ll think about how the “dinosaurs” of our time—massive corporations, human-centric industries, individual geniuses—might find themselves humbled. Are we, as individuals, destined to become the roosters pecking around the edges of a city designed for someone else’s purposes? Or is there a way to rewrite the rules before that future settles in?

3. Naming the Symptoms

Up close, all of this just looks like habit.

A phone in the hand.
A tab opened “for a second.”
A late-night session that runs longer than planned because one more track, one more clip, one more chord progression feels easier than standing up and turning it off.

From the inside, it feels like a thousand tiny, reasonable choices: staying informed, staying creative, staying connected, staying productive. None of them, on their own, seem like a crisis. It’s only when you zoom out that the pattern becomes visible.

What I’ve been calling doomscrolling and doom-creating are two faces of the same loop: a nervous system overclocked by systems that don’t care whether you’re nourished, only whether you’re engaged.

On the treadmill, that loop takes the form of my feet and thumb moving in sync. In the Suno k-hole, it takes the form of my imagination riding shotgun while the model drives. In both cases, there’s a point where the body starts to send small protest signals—stiff neck, dry eyes, a weird hollow feeling behind the sternum—and the software just keeps going.

The “dead internet” isn’t dead because nothing moves there. It’s dead because it no longer notices the difference between a living body and a tired one.

Burnout in a world that never admits it’s tired

In older stories about burnout, the script is pretty simple: too many demands, not enough time, a crash. The cure is also simple, at least in theory: rest, “unplugging,” a vacation somewhere without Wi-Fi.

In the AI era, that story feels off.

The demands have multiplied, but they now arrive disguised as opportunities:

you could write ten times faster with a model,

you could make an entire EP in a weekend with Suno,

you could storyboard a film in a day with image generators,

you could optimize your diet, your sleep, your workout, your finances with a swarm of micro-tools.

The pressure is no longer just “meet this deadline” but “what’s wrong with you if you’re not doing more with this power?”

My burnout doesn’t come from a boss shouting at me. It comes from an invisible layer of comparison: comparing myself not just to other humans, but to the pace of systems that never get tired, never get distracted, never have a bad day.

When I’m walking on the doomscroll treadmill or mass-generating songs, I’m not just using tools. I’m measuring myself against them.

There is always a faster version of me available in the browser:
one that replies quicker, drafts better emails, generates five moodboards instead of one, produces thirty tracks instead of three. That ghost version of myself becomes the new baseline. Anything less feels like slacking.

That’s a new flavor of burnout:

not just physical and emotional exhaustion,

but a chronic sense of being less than your own augmented shadow.

No vacation fixes that. You can’t unplug from a standard you’ve internalized.

Micro-motions, macro-logic

If I strip away the language and just list the motions my body makes, they’re almost embarrassingly small:

reach for phone

swipe

click tab

type prompt

press “generate”

nod, frown, repeat

Individually, these gestures are nothing. Your thumb flicking your feed is not a moral failure. My fingers hitting “generate again” are not the end of the world.

But when you string those gestures together day after day, year after year, you get something like a choreography of our era: a dance built from micro-movements that map directly onto the larger logic of the systems we’re in.

Doomscrolling expresses an economy that monetizes your attention by fragmenting it.
Doom-creating expresses an economy that monetizes your creativity by turning it into endless, low-friction output.

In both cases, your value to the system increases as you drift away from deep, sustained, bounded experiences and toward shallow, continuous ones.

Deep: one song, made slowly, lived with for months.
Shallow: a hundred songs, made quickly, lived with for five seconds each.

Deep: one careful conversation in person.
Shallow: fifty half-glimpsed clips, comments, interactions, reactions.

It’s not that one is pure and the other evil. It’s that the system we’re in has a clear preference. The ad server and the engagement dashboard know what they like. And we, over time, start to move in ways that please them.

My artworks in this space—Doomscrolling Treadmill, Doomscrolling Marathon, soon maybe an installation built from the Suno tracks—are my way of freezing those micro-motions long enough to look at them.

They ask:

What happens when we treat these gestures as choreography instead of “just how it is”?

What changes when we stage them in a gallery or festival, with a beginning and an end, instead of letting them bleed endlessly into our days?

What do we see in our own bodies when the loop is framed, not invisible?

Sometimes the answer is just: we see how tired we are.
But that’s already something.

The rooster on the sidewalk

The other day, walking through Miami, I saw a rooster picking at the edge of the sidewalk. It’s a common enough sight here that people barely notice. But for a moment I flashed to its ancestry.

This nervous, skinny bird is a distant relative of dinosaurs: creatures so large and powerful we still make blockbuster films about them. They were, for millions of years, the dominant force on land. Then the climate shifted, the world changed, and now their descendants weave between cars and storefronts, dodging trash and tourists.

There’s something humbling in that image: power scaled down, myth reduced to a nuisance in a parking lot.

I don’t think the future is going to be as simple as “AI wipes us out and we become roosters.” But I do think there’s a risk of a softer version of that story:

a world where a small number of actors own and steer vast AI systems,

where most people live in the margins of that arrangement,

where our role looks less like “dominant species” and more like background fauna coexisting with infrastructure we don’t control.

In that version of the story, we’re still here. We still work, love, make art, eat, raise kids, post memes. But the core levers of intelligence, planning, and large-scale decision-making sit somewhere else: in opaque stacks, in corporate clouds, in superstructures that feel as distant and untouchable as the weather.

When I see the rooster, I wonder:

If humans are the dinosaurs of this era, what is the climate shift we’re walking into?
And will we notice, or will it just feel like “the way things are now”?

In the context of this chapter, that question isn’t about apocalypse. It’s about sensitivity.

Can we feel, in our own bodies, when our movements start to look less like agency and more like adaptation to someone else’s design?

The way we scroll.

The way we create.

The way we sit at the workstation feeling like there is always more we should be doing.

Those are not just personal quirks. They’re evolutionary responses to a pressure we didn’t choose but now live under.

The rooster isn’t thinking about dinosaurs. It’s just trying to survive the day. We may end up in a similar position—so busy adapting to the latest tool, the latest platform, the latest “must-have” AI—that we lose sight of how much power has quietly migrated away from the flesh and into the stack.

That’s why, for me, the body has to stay central in this book.

Theory can tell us that capitalism is an atmosphere and AI is an infrastructure. But it’s the body that tells us when that atmosphere is getting thin. It’s the body that signals when an environment is becoming unlivable, even if the dashboard still says everything is fine.

When my legs ache after a long treadmill performance, that’s data.
When my brain feels jangly after a Suno binge, that’s data.
When my hand keeps reaching for the phone even though nothing good has happened there for hours, that’s data.

Not scientific data. Lived data.

In later chapters, we’ll talk about how this plays out in cities, in churches, in classrooms, in families. We’ll talk about AI slop, vectoralist classes, techno-spirituality, and children of the utility. But none of that matters if we skip this layer: the simple acknowledgement that something in us is straining under the pace and logic of a machine that promises everything and quietly asks for our nervous systems in return.

So before we move on, I want to mark a few things as facts—not in the literal, empirical sense, but in the sense of things I trust because my body has repeated them enough:

I know what it feels like to move a lot and not go anywhere.

I know what it feels like to create a lot and not feel more alive.

I know what it feels like to be surrounded by endless content and still feel starved for meaning.

Those are the ground truths of “dead internet, live body” for me.

The rest of the book is an attempt to figure out what those truths imply:
about how we design tools, about how we teach each other, about how we build rituals and artworks that don’t just decorate the machine, but help us live inside it without forgetting that we’re more than roosters.

4. From Body to City

If the last pages have stayed very close to the body—thumbs, eyes, lungs, feet—it’s because that’s where the story starts for me. Before “AI slop,” before “platform capitalism,” before “vectoralist class,” there’s a person on a treadmill with a phone, or in a chair with a laptop, doing their best to keep up.

But the body is never really alone.

The gestures I’ve been describing—scrolling, generating, clicking, refreshing—are not private quirks. They’re interfaces between flesh and larger systems:

your thumb on the glass is also a finger on an ad marketplace,

your playlist is also training data,

your prompt is also a micro-request to a cluster of GPUs humming in a distant warehouse,

your treadmill performance is also a trace inside a city that’s already wired for logistics, tourism, and spectacle.

The “dead internet” we feel in our nervous systems is inseparable from the live infrastructures that surround us: cables, antennas, server farms, delivery fleets, payment rails, zoning laws, real-estate spreadsheets, moderation rules, content policies.

The body is the front line; the city is the battlefield.

The city as extended nervous system

I live and work in Miami, a place that often gets marketed as sunshine, nightlife, and escape—but which also functions as a very specific kind of node in planetary computation:

a logistics and shipping hub,

a real-estate speculation playground,

a crypto experiment site,

an art fair brand,

a tourism engine,

a climate risk zone.

On the street, that stack looks like:

food delivery robots weaving around roosters,

LED billboards pushing finance apps and luxury towers,

tourists recording content for feeds that run on recommendation engines elsewhere,

Uber drivers following routes optimized by invisible software,

people like me walking to the studio with phones buzzing in their pockets and GPU jobs running somewhere in the cloud.

If my body feels overclocked, it’s partly because the city is overclocked.

Miami runs hot—economically, climatically, emotionally. It’s full of extremes: wealth and precarity, development and decay, hyper-connected nightlife and infrastructural neglect. That intensity makes it a perfect place to think about what happens when a “dead internet” spills out of screens and starts shaping streets, skylines, and daily routines.

When I put a treadmill in a gallery or festival space here, I’m not importing a foreign problem. I’m amplifying a local one:

the grind economy,

the hustle myth,

the idea that if you stop moving—financially, socially, aesthetically—you’ll be left behind.

The doomscroll is not just an individual failure of discipline. It’s a nervous micro-response to a macro-environment that’s constantly whispering more.

Slop in the wild

Soon, in later chapters, I’ll talk about AI slop—Kate Crawford’s term for the avalanche of low-quality synthetic content generated to game algorithms, fill feeds, and optimize engagement.

For now, I just want to mark that the slop is not confined to the rectangle of the screen. It leaks.

You can see it in:

generic-looking “luxury” architecture that might as well have been rendered by a diffusion model,

cafes and co-working spaces that all copy the same Pinterest aesthetics,

influencer photo spots that turn entire neighborhoods into backdrops,

branded experiences whose primary function is to be photographed and reposted.

Slop is a metabolic condition: input, processing, waste. We feed data into the machine; it digests it; it excretes content and optimization strategies; we live inside the residue.

As AI generation scales, that metabolism accelerates. We move from:

people posting content to feeds,
→ to people collaborating with models to flood feeds,
→ to bots and models generating content for other bots and models,
→ to cities subtly rearranged around the incentives of those bots and models.

A “dead internet city” is one where the dominant aesthetics, incentive structures, and even urban rhythms are shaped less by the needs of residents and more by the needs of platforms and capital.

It’s a place where:

architecture is optimized for Airbnb photos,

murals are optimized for selfies,

schedules are optimized for engagement,

and public space is increasingly carved into rentable, brandable moments.

In that kind of environment, the tired body on the treadmill isn’t an anomaly. It’s a preview.

Art as a local glitch

So where does art fit into this?

When I build works like Doomscrolling Treadmill, Doomscrolling Marathon, Smart Shoppers, Simulation Faith, or future pieces around AI music and slop, I’m not trying to sit above this mess as a neutral observer. I’m inside it. I’m one more node in the network, one more human trying to make sense of a stack that wasn’t designed for human sense-making.

What art can still do—when it’s honest—is create localized glitches in the climate:

It can slow down gestures that are usually automatic and make them visible.

It can reframe habits as choreography, infrastructure as stage design, exhaustion as signal.

It can insert doubt and reflection into systems that are optimized for speed and certainty.

Putting a treadmill in a gallery doesn’t fix doomscrolling.
It does something smaller and, to me, valuable: it turns an unconscious loop into a scene.

You can watch someone walk in place for an hour, scrolling, sweating, zoning out. You can feel the absurdity in your gut. You might recognize yourself. You might laugh, or feel uneasy, or feel nothing at all. But at least, for a moment, the loop is framed.

My hope is that as we move through the rest of this book, the artworks act like anchors in the middle of dense concepts:

dead internet ↔ live body ↔ live city,

AI slop ↔ urban metabolism,

vectoralist class ↔ everyday hustles,

techno-spirituality ↔ Miami storefront churches,

children of the utility ↔ the next generation’s nervous systems.

The works don’t explain these systems. They touch them.

They’re the points where theory meets sweat, cables, resin, grass patches, plastic baby Jesuses, skeletons wrapped in Bolívares, shopping carts full of 3D-printed brains, and improvised GPU rigs humming in the corner.

Stepping off (for a moment)

At the end of a treadmill session, there’s always a weird moment when you step off. Your legs keep feeling like they’re moving even when you’re standing on solid ground. Your body has to recalibrate to a world that isn’t sliding under you.

Finishing this chapter feels a bit like that.

We’ve been walking in place—doomscroll, doom-create, repeat—trying to feel what a “dead internet” does to a still-living body. Now it’s time to step off the treadmill and look around.

In the next part of this project, the camera will pull back:

first to the metabolism of slop—how generative content circulates, piles up, and begins to feed back into itself,

then to cities like Miami as nodes in a planetary stack of computation, capital, and culture,

and then to the spiritual and generational dimensions of living inside this arrangement.

We’ll talk about vectoralist classes, AI slop, techno-spiritual churches, kids raised on utilities of intelligence. We’ll walk through streets, galleries, labs, churches, and feeds, using my own works as reference points and other thinkers as translators.

But I want to carry something forward from this chapter:

Whatever we end up saying about systems, classes, stacks, or slop,
it only matters if it stays accountable to the reality of the bodies living inside them.

If a theory about “dead internet cities” doesn’t line up with how it feels to walk down the street in Miami, or to sit at a workstation at 11:47 p.m., or to lie in bed and auto-play one more video, then it’s missing something.

So as we head into the next chapters, think of your own body as a kind of sensor:

Notice when your breathing changes as you read about slop and stack.

Notice when your shoulders tense thinking about work, or money, or AI, or the future.

Notice when your thumb reaches for your phone without you deciding to.

Those micro-movements are part of the story. They’re the reason any of this is worth writing about at all.

The internet may be “dead” in some abstract, structural way.
But you are not.

The question, moving forward, is how to inhabit that gap—between dead systems and live bodies—in a way that doesn’t just exhaust us, but opens up new ways of seeing, relating, and, occasionally, refusing.
