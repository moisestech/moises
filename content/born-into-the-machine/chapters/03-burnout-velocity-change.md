○ GPT Answer: Short answer: nobody knows—but there are some pretty consistent trajectories in the research that line up eerily well with the stuff you’re already sensing (intelligence as commodity, techno-spirituality, dead internet / dead smart cities, etc.).

I’ll give you a high-level “next 50 years after ChatGPT” map in 7 zones, but keep it grounded in what’s reasonably well-supported vs pure sci-fi.

1. Intelligence fully becomes a utility

Trajectory already visible now:

AI is moving from “cool feature” to infrastructure: embedded in office suites, phones, dev tools, logistics, etc.

Frontier models are getting better, cheaper per token, and more multimodal, which is exactly what you’d expect from scaling laws + hardware trends (even if Moore’s law slows, specialized chips + clusters keep pushing).

50-year-ish implications:

“Being smart” becomes like “having electricity” — it’s weird not to have some access to on-demand cognition.

The real divide becomes:

those who design/own large cognitive infrastructures (model providers, infra, data pipelines),

those who are adept orchestrators (prompt + toolchain fluency, like you),

and those who are mainly modeled (their labor/data/behavior feeds the systems but they don’t direct them much).

This directly matches your instinct: intelligence as commodity. And yeah, I think in 50 years that’ll feel as normal as paying a power bill.

2. Work, class & “prompt economics”

What research suggests:

Studies already show automation doesn’t just “kill jobs,” it recomposes tasks—highly automatable tasks get stripped out; humans move to coordination, care, creativity, oversight. (Think WEF / OECD style analyses.)

The people who benefit most are usually those with capital + complementary skills (managers, creatives, engineers), not low-wage workers without bargaining power.

Likely pattern over 50 years:

A chunk of “knowledge work” becomes a thin human layer atop thick AI stacks: editing, curating, arbitrating, prompting.

There’s serious pressure to introduce some mix of:

UBI / negative income tax style supports, and/or

aggressive retraining + public AI access,
especially as entire job families (call centers, basic back office tasks, low-tier content creation) get hollowed out.

This is where your “Home Ec for AI” / prompting-as-literacy idea lands: states or cities that don’t teach AI-orchestration early basically manufacture a cognitive underclass.

3. Cities: from “smart” to “dead smart” layers

Already visible trends:

More cameras, sensors, delivery robots, dynamic pricing, algorithmic policing, digital twins, etc.

But also: empty malls, failing downtowns, “15-minute city” experiments, and suburban sprawl.

50-year extrapolation:

Core metros (NYC, Miami, LA, London, Shenzhen, etc.) keep a stacked reality:

Physical city

IoT / logistics city (robots, drones, autonomous fleets)

Data/behavioral city (ads, scoring, personalization)

Some districts stay hyper animated; others become “dead smart zones”: fully instrumented but sparsely human—billboards, sensors, and bots still running because contracts + infra outlived actual social use.

That’s your dead internet / dead city bleed-through: bots talking to bots in real space. You’re already seeing sketches of that in Miami with delivery carts, scooters, vacant branded districts, etc.

4. Belief systems: techno-spirituality normalizes

Signals already here:

Algorithmic recommendation already acts as a kind of soft liturgy: daily rituals, streaks, moral outrage cycles.

People turn to models for therapy, journaling, prayer/affirmations, existential questions.

Likely evolution:

AI “pastors” / guides become completely normal: apps/models trained on theology + CBT + wellness that deliver ultra-personal spiritual content.

Hybrid institutions:

Small physical communities,

plus big, networked, AI-mediated “congregations” across platforms.

Data as devotion: time, sentiment, and money given to these systems measured on dashboards (“faith engagement analytics”).

You’re early in framing this as techno-spiritual churches—in 50 years, it’s very plausible there are denominations that start as apps and only later buy buildings.

5. Media, aesthetics & “slop”

Trends now:

Text-to-image/video (Sora-type tools) making cinematic output trivial.

Feeds already optimize ruthlessly for watch time/engagement.

50-year picture:

The line between Hollywood, TikTok, and “AI slop” basically dissolves. Everything is high-production by default.

Status moves away from “polish” to:

scarce aesthetics (glitch, delay, analog weirdness, human failure),

and scarce contexts (live, local, embodied).

You get attention castes:

some people swim mostly in short, hyper-personal AI feeds;

others in curated “slow media” (like a future Criterion / Mubi);

others in immersive, always-on worlds.

Your “velocity aesthetics” notion becomes crucial: contemporary art and design become a translation layer helping humans metabolize speeds and complexity they can’t otherwise grasp.

6. Demography, kids as “luxury,” and care

What’s fairly solid:

Many wealthy countries are already seeing falling birth rates, aging populations, and rising care burdens.

Child-rearing costs (time + money) keep rising in cities; a lot of younger people feel like kids are economically out of reach.

Forward trajectory:

Having children in dense, expensive cities does become a class marker: you need not just money, but “attention wealth” to resist pure screen-based childcare.

For many, care becomes hybrid:

AI co-parents (tutors, emotional companions, health monitors),

plus a mix of gig workers / institutions.

Parallel tracks:

AI-raised kids: lots of time with synthetic agents, optimized media, datafied from birth.

Boutique analog kids: restricted screens, curated environments, artisanal schooling.

Your “UBI + kids as luxury + AI childcare” speculation is very much in line with where contemporary demography + tech trends point.

7. Governance, rights & the weird stuff

Some medium-risk, medium-likelihood things that are widely discussed in serious circles:

Strong fights over:

AI labor displacement and unions,

data rights (who owns your behavior / likeness),

synthetic media and democracy (election interference, deep fakes, narrative control).

Regulatory regimes diverge:

Some blocs go heavy on control (surveillance + state AI monopoles).

Others lean into open ecosystems with guardrails.

Concepts like:

“Rights of the modeled” (what can be done with your face, voice, or style?),

“Cognitive safety” (protecting people from predatory personalization),
move from niche to mainstream.

Existential “AGI/ASI” scenarios are debated, but from a practice standpoint, the more certain path is: AI becomes background infrastructure for nearly everything—and we fight political battles over who steers it and who it serves.
