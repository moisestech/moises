Chapter 3 – Slop and Sacrament: Notes from a Dead Internet City
1. The Feed That Started to Taste Wrong

There was a point where the internet stopped feeling like a stream and started feeling like a buffet that had been left out too long.

At first, I couldn’t name it. I’d open a social app and see the usual mix: friends’ posts, memes, ads, news clips, thirst traps, tutorials. But slowly, something about the texture changed. The edges got blurry. The content felt… undercooked.

You’ve probably felt it too:

The voiceovers with the same slightly off pacing.

The “Top 10 Things You Didn’t Know About…” scripts that all sound like they came from the same ghostwriter.

The AI faces, almost perfect but with that faint plasticity in the eyes.

The “watch till the end” clips whose endings don’t justify the time.

The swarm of NPC-style streams and recycled “fact” accounts.

One day I scrolled for twenty minutes and realized I could barely remember a single thing I’d seen. Not because it was shocking or traumatic—just because it was all vaguely similar, vaguely optimized, vaguely hollow. It was like eating spoonfuls of sauce with no actual meal.

That’s around when the word “slop” started circulating online: a rude way of naming content that’s cheap, low-effort, and designed only to keep the engagement loop going. With AI models now in the mix, slop has gone from a style to a metabolism:

scrape → generate → post → measure → tweak → repeat.

Underneath the doomscroll and the doom-creating, there’s a bigger process humming along—one that doesn’t really care whether any particular clip, track, or image is meaningful, as long as the overall flow doesn’t stop.

I started noticing how often my body reacted to that flow like it was eating junk food:

A quick hit of interest,

followed by a kind of mental bloating,

followed by a low-key shame or emptiness,

followed by the impulse to take another bite anyway.

The “dead internet” stopped being an abstract conspiracy theory and started feeling like a diet problem. Not just for me, but for whole cities.

Miami as a stomach

When I walk through Miami now, especially in neighborhoods like Wynwood or downtown, I can’t unsee the slop logic.

There are murals that feel like they exist purely as Instagram backdrops. Cafés that look like they were interior-designed by the same Pinterest board. Airbnbs staged for photos more than for living. Pop-up experiences whose real purpose is to be filmed, clipped, and fed back into the content cycle.

The city starts to look like a digestive system for images:

global aesthetics in,

recombined and filtered through local branding,

then excreted as photos, stories, reels, shorts.

Some of that is fun. I’m not above enjoying a colorful wall or a well-lit latte. But when you start stacking AI slop on top of that already image-saturated environment, something shifts.

You get:

AI-generated real-estate renders selling towers that don’t exist yet,

AI-styled festival flyers,

AI voiceovers announcing AI-made clips shot against real Miami backdrops,

generative “visions of the future city” that look suspiciously like the condos already going up.

The boundary between representation and construction gets fuzzy. The slop doesn’t just describe the city; it begins to nudge how the city is built, painted, and sold.

I catch myself in the middle of it, phone in hand, half-documenting, half-participating. Artist, consumer, generator, critic—all in one scrolling body.

When the holy and the cheap touch

The word “sacrament” has been in the back of my mind as I’ve watched this happen.

In a religious context, a sacrament is a visible sign of an invisible grace—something simple and material that carries a charge: bread, wine, water, ash, a candle, a gesture. The power isn’t in the material itself; it’s in the attention, belief, and ritual wrapped around it.

Slop is the opposite. It’s material with almost no charge.

It’s made to be skimmed, swiped past, half-heard. It’s optimized not for depth, but for throughput. The point isn’t to change you—it’s to keep you present enough, long enough, for the metric to register.

And yet, weirdly, these two realms touch.

I’ve seen low-res Jesus memes on WhatsApp travel farther and mean more to people than high-budget religious films. I’ve seen TikToks about crystals, astrology, manifestation, conspiracy, and techno-spirituality function as tiny sermons for algorithmic congregations. I’ve seen AI-generated “angel” art gather real prayer in the comments.

Even in the sloppiest parts of the feed, people are still looking for something sacred—comfort, connection, a sign, a pattern, a promise.

This is where the “dead internet city” gets complicated:

On one hand, the metabolism of AI slop is clearly extractive and flattening. It eats nuance and spits out engagement.

On the other hand, people keep trying to smuggle meaning through that same machinery—posting grief, hope, rituals, testimonies, jokes that are really cries for help.

What do you call it when a system built for slop keeps accidentally hosting tiny sacraments?

That’s the tension this chapter wants to sit in.

From feed to altar

When I make a piece like Simulation Faith—a baby Jesus with a VR headset—or when I imagine entrances to future AI churches, I’m not just being ironic. I’m trying to mark a fact:

The same infrastructures that deliver slop are already delivering belief.

The recommendation engine that sends you prank videos also sends you sermons, conspiracies, affirmations, meditations, manifestos. The same models that generate infinite anime girls can generate infinite saints. The same stacks that optimize e-commerce optimize spiritual content.

The dead internet has hot spots of devotion.

Some kid in Hialeah might be watching a generative “Bible story” channel with AI voices. Some abuela might forward a chain-message prayer image with glitchy JPEG compression and Comic Sans text. Some crypto bro might listen to an AI-laundered motivational guru telling him the market is “energy.”

The city’s spiritual life is slowly, quietly, getting rerouted through slop tunnels.

When I walk past a small storefront church in Miami—LED sign, plastic chairs, drums, cheap PA system—I think about how their message now competes not just with other churches but with TikTok witches, YouTube pastors, AI prophetic bots, and algorithmically boosted cults of personality.

They’re all drawing from the same pool of attention.
They’re all swimming in the same dead internet sea.

The question for me as an artist isn’t:
Is that good or bad?

It’s more like:

How do we even see this metabolism clearly?

What does it feel like, on the ground, in a body and in a neighborhood?

What kinds of images and installations can expose its shape without becoming just another layer of slop?

In the next sections of this chapter, I’ll zoom in on that metabolism more directly:

how AI slop actually circulates,

how it changes the look and rhythm of cities like Miami,

and how an artwork can operate as either another calorie in the system or a moment of fasting—a pause in the feed, a different kind of attention.

For now, I want to hold onto a simple observation:

There’s a point where the feed starts to taste wrong,
and that taste is a clue.

It’s a clue that something in the metabolism has shifted from nourishment to mere intake.
It’s a clue that our nervous systems are picking up on a mismatch: too much input, not enough meaning.
It’s a clue that even inside the slop, people are still reaching for the sacred—however distorted, commodified, or low-res it arrives.

“Dead internet city” doesn’t mean the city is literally dead. It means the main circulation system of images, sounds, and stories has stopped caring whether we come away more alive.

In that environment, any moment of genuine presence—any small sacrament of attention—already feels like resistance.

The rest of this chapter is about tracking that metabolism more concretely, and figuring out where, if anywhere, we can place a hand on the gears.

2. The Metabolism of Slop

Once you start thinking of AI slop as a metabolism, it’s hard to unsee the organs.

There’s an intake phase: scraping the web, recording behavior, logging every click, scroll, pause, and purchase.
There’s a digestion phase: training models, tuning them, optimizing them to predict the next token, the next frame, the next beat.
There’s an excretion phase: generating millions of clips, images, tracks, posts—most of which no one remembers a day later.
And then there’s re-ingestion: all that output gets scraped again, folded back into datasets, used to tune the next generation of models.

Input → model → output → feedback.
Eat → process → shit → eat again.

It’s grotesque and elegant at the same time. A closed loop of inference and exhaust.

The thing about a metabolism is: it doesn’t care about individual molecules. It cares about flow. Whether you, personally, got something nourishing from a particular piece of content is almost irrelevant to the system, as long as the overall circulation continues.

From that perspective, my doomscrolling and doom-creating are just micro-movements inside a much bigger digestive process. The city, too, becomes part of the gut.

Data as calories

Every time you:

like a post,

hover on a video for more than a second,

replay a sound,

type a prompt,

linger on an image,

you’re feeding the metabolism. You’re supplying a tiny bit of information about what keeps you there. The system doesn’t need to understand why you stayed, only that you stayed.

Those micro-calories of attention get aggregated and transformed into:

weights in a neural network,

A/B test results,

tweaks to recommendation policies,

ad-targeting segments.

Then, when you open the app again, you’re eating the processed version of what you fed it last week.

AI just accelerates this loop and makes it more literal. The model is trained on oceans of data—some consensual, some scraped, some stolen, some volunteered. It compresses that into a structure that can predict patterns at scale. Then it spits out new content that resembles what it’s seen, but remixed, stretched, hallucinated.

We call that “generation.”

In metabolic terms, it’s closer to regurgitation.

It’s the stomach burping up a new configuration of flavors it has already ingested 10,000 times. Sometimes the burp is sublime. Often it’s just more gas.

When the volume of that regurgitation becomes enormous—thousands of images, clips, and tracks per second, across millions of users—you get what feels like a slop flood: a thickening layer of content that’s hard to distinguish, hard to care about, and hard to opt out of.

You can feel that flood at the level of the feed, but you can also feel it at the level of the city.

Smart Shoppers: brains in the cart

That’s where Smart Shoppers lives for me.

A shopping cart stuffed with glowing 3D-printed brains: it’s a simple image, almost cartoonish. But it’s also a metabolic diagram. Cognition—symbolized by the brains—has become a commodity. It’s stacked, piled, and pushed around inside a consumer object designed to move goods through a store.

In the age of AI slop, our attention and intelligence are not just being exploited; they’re being treated as raw materials and packaged products at the same time.

On one level, we are the shoppers:

pushing our carts through the endless aisles of the internet,

selecting content, tweaking prompts, filling our mental baskets.

On another level, we are the items:

our reactions, preferences, and behaviors are the brains in someone else’s cart,

being moved through advertising pipelines, training pipelines, optimization pipelines.

Smart Shoppers makes that inversion visible:

The brains aren’t safely housed in skulls; they’re merchandise.

The cart isn’t carrying groceries; it’s carrying cognitive potential, ready to be sold, analyzed, or consumed.

In the context of slop metabolism, the piece reads almost like a still from inside the gut:

Here is the payload the system is really moving:
not just goods, but our capacity to think, feel, and decide.

AI tools promise to amplify that capacity—“augment your intelligence,” “boost your creativity”—but they also enlist it. They tap and extract it, turning our prompts, corrections, and experiments into free labor that makes the model more valuable for its owners.

That’s the thing about a metabolism: it’s always metabolizing something. In this case, it’s metabolizing us.

E-waste floor: the colon

Another image that haunts me is the e-waste floor—that shimmering sea of CDs, cables, and obsolete electronics spread across a gallery floor. It’s a piece you’ve been circling in your mind: a literal landscape of discarded storage, reflecting light, shifting underfoot, making it hard to walk.

If Smart Shoppers is the shopping phase of the metabolism, the e-waste floor is the colon.

It’s the part of the system where the leftovers accumulate:

old media formats,

outdated devices,

dead hard drives,

broken chargers,

plastic remnants of past upgrades.

In a slop-based internet, where generation is cheap and storage is vast, we rarely see this layer. Our phones pretend data is weightless. The cloud pretends to have no geography. You scroll a feed and never have to step on the landfill.

The e-waste floor forces a collision:

The same CDs that once carried precious content—music, films, software—become a slippery, slightly dangerous terrain.

The viewer has to negotiate every step, suddenly aware of the material consequences of “immaterial” media.

It’s a quiet, glittering horror: beautiful and toxic at the same time.

Placed in the context of AI slop, it points to a future where:

the volume of generated content far exceeds our capacity to absorb it,

and yet all of it still leaves a trace—servers, cooling systems, discarded devices, carbon emissions, psychological residue.

The metabolism doesn’t just vanish what it can’t use. It reroutes it into waste pools—physical, mental, environmental.

Doomscrolling Treadmill: the circulatory system

If Smart Shoppers is a digestive organ and the e-waste floor is a colon, Doomscrolling Treadmill is the circulatory system.

It keeps things moving:

The belt under your feet, the feed under your thumb, the endless cycling of content through your eyes and nervous system.

It doesn’t store or digest; it pumps.

From the system’s perspective, your doomscroll is a way of keeping the slop in motion. Each swipe sends a tiny signal: more of this, less of that. Each watch-time metric nudges the next batch of content. Each comment feeds another micro-debate.

You are the blood cell, carrying bits of data through the network.

The treadmill makes that role absurdly literal. You are, quite literally, powering the circulation with your body. You’re the motor and the cargo.

You could imagine other existing or future pieces as organs too:

Simulation Faith as a gland secreting meaning into the network.

Price of Existence as a calcified tumor of value and decay.

Meditation Battlestation as an endocrine hack—RGB hormones pulsing through a stressed-out nervous system trying to relax inside a gamer aesthetic.

Taken together, your practice becomes a kind of anatomy lesson for the slop metabolism.

Not a diagram on a whiteboard, but a walkthrough in real space:

carts, bones, babies, headsets, screens, fake grass, water jugs, printed gradients, servers, masks, and treadmills, all playing their part.

Who’s eating whom?

The unsettling question underneath all of this is deceptively simple:

In this metabolism, who is really being nourished?

The platforms and their owners clearly are:

they extract value from the data, the engagement, the generative workflows,

they sell access to the models as services,

they convert attention into revenue and control.

Some artists and users, like you, also get real nourishment:

you discover new forms,

you accelerate your experiments,

you build communities and infrastructures that wouldn’t exist without these tools.

But there’s a point where the metabolic metaphor turns dark:

The system feeds us slop to keep us present.

We feed the system data to keep it learning.

The “full” feeling we get—of being busy, engaged, entertained—may hide a deeper malnutrition.

You can make 300 songs in a weekend and still feel empty.
You can scroll for two hours and remember nothing.
You can watch your city glow with screens and feel less oriented than ever.

In that sense, we’re not just diners in the AI cafeteria; we’re also livestock.

We’re being fattened on content that keeps us docile and predictable, while the real feast—the value extracted from our behavior—happens elsewhere, in boardrooms and datacenters.

That sounds dramatic, but at the micro-level it plays out in small ways:

putting off sleep to finish “one more thing,”

accepting spammy engagement hacks as normal,

designing your own work to fit algorithmic preferences,

tweaking your self-presentation to be more “brand-friendly,”

feeling guilty if you’re not “leveraging” the latest model.

That’s why it matters to talk about slop as a metabolism and not just a trend. Trends pass. Metabolisms persist. They shape bodies, cities, and cultures over time.

Art as metabolic awareness

If we’re stuck, for now, inside this digestive system, the question becomes:

What can art do inside a metabolism it can’t immediately escape?

I don’t think art can heal the system, or “fix” slop, or single-handedly redirect the flow of capital. That’s too much weight to put on any practice.

But art can do at least three modest but important things:

Make the organs visible.
Turn invisible processes—scraping, training, wasting, circulating—into images and structures you can walk around, sit with, or be confronted by.

Slow the digestion.
Create moments where, instead of consuming ten things in ten seconds, you have to stay with one thing longer than the metabolism prefers.

Introduce indigestion.
Not in a cruel way, but in a way that makes the system glitch: a work that doesn’t fit cleanly into a category, that doesn’t perform well as slop, that resists easy clipping or reduces engagement instead of maximizing it.

Smart Shoppers, Doomscrolling Treadmill, the e-waste floor, Simulation Faith—each of these has the potential to function as a little metabolic disturbance. They remind us that something is being eaten and excreted at scale, and that our bodies are involved, whether we consent or not.

In the next section, we’ll bring in some of the theorists who’ve given language to this metabolism—people writing about AI slop, the Stack, and vectoralist power—and place your Miami practice inside that frame.

Not to bury the work under theory, but to connect what you’re already making to a wider map of how intelligence, images, and cities are being reorganized.

Because understanding the metabolism is the first step toward deciding, even in small ways, how much of yourself you’re willing to feed it.

3. Crawford, Bratton, Wark: Naming the Slop Stack

When I talk about slop as a metabolism, I’m not doing it alone. There are people who’ve been anatomizing this thing from different angles for years. Three of them—Kate Crawford, Benjamin Bratton, and McKenzie Wark—have become like lenses I keep swapping onto the same camera.

They don’t explain my work, but they sharpen the outline of the environment my work lives in.

Kate Crawford: slop as waste and fuel

In her essay “Eating the Future: The Metabolic Logic of AI Slop,” Kate Crawford describes the flood of synthetic AI content as part of a wider transformation: generative systems turn human culture into a vast extractive resource, then pump out images, text, and video that are increasingly detached from lived reality. Slop, for her, is not just a glitchy aesthetic; it’s waste that also becomes fuel—a feedback loop where low-quality output gets scraped back into datasets, training the next wave of models.

She borrows from environmental theory’s idea of a “metabolic rift”—the way capitalist production breaks natural cycles—to argue that AI introduces a cultural metabolic rift: it consumes our archives, our labor, our attention, and spits out synthetic goop that smothers the territory instead of mapping it.

That image lines up almost perfectly with what I feel when the feed “starts to taste wrong.” The reason it feels like eating empty calories is because, at a structural level, that’s exactly what’s happening: the system optimizes for circulation, not nourishment.

The Suno k-hole is a micro-version of this: I feed prompts in, it burps tracks out, and the more I generate, the more training data I contribute to some future model.

The Doomscrolling Treadmill is the circulatory system: my thumb and feet keep the slop flowing, turning my attention into metadata.

Smart Shoppers and the e-waste floor are snapshots of input and waste: brains-as-merchandise, hardware husks glittering like fossilized shit.

Crawford’s language—slop as a metabolic regime, not just an aesthetic—gives me a way to think about these pieces as organs in a body that’s bigger than me.

I’m not just parodying bad content. I’m tracing the digestion.

Benjamin Bratton: the Stack as accidental church

If Crawford gives us the guts, Benjamin Bratton gives us the skeleton.

In The Stack: On Software and Sovereignty, he argues that planetary-scale computation has accidentally assembled itself into a kind of megastructure: six interlocking layers—Earth, Cloud, City, Address, Interface, User—through which power, information, and governance now flow.

Earth: mines, minerals, energy, e-waste.

Cloud: data centers, platforms, corporate infrastructures.

City: smart urban systems, sensors, logistics, real estate.

Address: IPs, user IDs, payment tokens, device IDs.

Interface: screens, dashboards, prompts, APIs.

User: us—humans, but also bots, agents, models.

We don’t live around this Stack; we live inside it, and it lives inside us. It’s a new architecture of sovereignty that runs parallel to nation-states.

When I think about Miami through Bratton’s model, it stops being just a backdrop and becomes a node:

On the Earth layer: rising seas, hurricanes, heat, and the literal sand everything is built on.

On the Cloud layer: AWS regions, content delivery networks, exchanges routing money through Florida entities, AI services I call from my studio.

On the City layer: delivery robots, Uber routes, surveillance cameras, festival logistics, condo towers sold via AI renders.

On the Address layer: tourists, migrants, artists, investors, all as points in databases.

On the Interface layer: the screens in Wynwood, the LED walls, the phones everyone holds up at Basel, the treadmill display in my performance.

On the User layer: me, you, roosters, bots, models, all coexisting as “users” in different ways.

My Doomscrolling Treadmill is basically a small, ridiculous Stack simulator: Earth (my body, the treadmill motor), Cloud (the platforms behind my feed), City (the festival or gallery hosting it in Miami), Address (my account, my metrics), Interface (phone, screen, belt), User (me as performer, the audience as watchers, the algorithm as co-user).

Seen through Bratton, the treadmill isn’t just a critique of scrolling; it’s an architectural cross-section of how planetary computation runs through a single body in a single city.

And when I imagine AI churches—storefront sanctuaries with LED signs and chatbots, Wynwood-style murals and language models doing sermon prep—they’re basically Stack chapels:

Earth: air conditioning, electricity, bodies.

Cloud: LLMs, hosting, finance.

City: zoning laws, neighbors, cops, landlords.

Address: donors, members, devices, wallets.

Interface: screens, mics, forms, WhatsApp groups.

User: believers, lurkers, bots, priests, agents.

Bratton’s Stack makes it clear that techno-spiritual futures aren’t just metaphors; they’re architectural possibilities baked into the layers we already inhabit.

McKenzie Wark: the vectoralist class and the rest of us

If Crawford talks about metabolism and Bratton about structure, McKenzie Wark talks about who benefits.

In texts like A Hacker Manifesto and later writing on what she calls the vectoralist class, Wark argues that information has created a new ruling class whose power comes from controlling the vectors—the channels and infrastructures—through which information flows.

Under industrial capitalism, the key struggle was between those who owned the means of production (capitalists) and those who sold their labor (workers). Under vectoralism, the crucial divide is between those who own and manage information infrastructures—stacks, platforms, networks—and those whose lives, data, and creativity are routed through them.

In that frame:

Big AI labs, cloud providers, and platform companies are vectoralists: they own the stacks, the models, the data centers, the “intelligence-as-a-service.”

Most of us—artists, coders, users, kids on TikTok, drivers, “creators”—are a kind of info-prole: we generate data, content, prompts, corrections, cultures that make those vectors valuable.

Wark’s point isn’t to say “everything is hopeless.” It’s to redraw the battle lines:

who owns the models,

who owns the training data,

who can turn slop into long-term power,

who’s stuck living in the wake of those decisions.

When I look at AI slop through Wark’s eyes, I don’t just see low-quality content. I see evidence of a class project:

Models trained on scraped culture.

Platforms monetizing the output as engagement.

Cloud companies billing for the compute.

A small layer of owners capturing most of the value.

Meanwhile, people like me:

pay to use these tools,

donate our prompts and corrections,

build artworks and small infrastructures on top of them,

try to leverage their speed to survive economically.

In other words: we’re doing the cognitive labor inside someone else’s factory.

My Smart Shoppers cart full of brains becomes, in Wark’s language, a portrait of the info-proletariat: cognition packaged, moved, priced. The brains could be users, artists, writers, coders, precarious creatives, all piled into a metal frame built for consumption in a store owned by someone else.

My treadmill pieces become portraits of the info-prole’s cardio: we keep the vectors alive with our movement, but we don’t set their direction.

Bringing Wark into this chapter helps me be honest about a tension in my own position:

I’m critical of the slop metabolism and the Stack…

while also using those same systems to make work, teach, pay rent, and write this book.

I’m not outside the factory; I’m a slightly self-aware worker on the floor.

Putting it together: where I’m standing

So if I combine these three lenses:

Crawford says: AI slop is a metabolic condition—waste as fuel, culture as resource, a rift between what gets produced and what nourishes us.

Bratton says: we live inside a Stack—Earth/Cloud/City/Address/Interface/User—and our cities, including Miami, are specific configurations of that megastructure.

Wark says: the Stack is run by a vectoralist class who owns the means of information, while the rest of us hack, scroll, and generate inside their vectors.

Where does that leave me, personally?

Roughly here:

I’m an artist and engineer living in a dead-internet city,
working inside a planetary Stack I don’t control,
contributing to a slop metabolism that both empowers and depletes me,
while trying to build small, local glitches that make these forces visible.

My pieces are not solutions. They’re diagnostic images:

Doomscrolling Treadmill: an MRI of attention as circulation.

Smart Shoppers: an X-ray of cognition as commodity.

E-waste floor: a CT scan of digital waste as terrain.

Simulation Faith + future AI church works: an ultrasound of belief moving through the Stack.

This chapter, with Crawford, Bratton, and Wark in the room, is my attempt to say:

the slop isn’t accidental; it’s structural,

the city isn’t separate from the internet; it’s one of its layers,

the weird exhaustion we feel isn’t just “personal”; it’s classed, infrastructural, and designed.

In the next chapter, I want to narrow the focus again and ask:

What happens when these same dynamics enter explicitly spiritual space—when the Stack learns to preach, when slop becomes scripture, when AI pastors and WhatsApp miracles start to feel normal?

And what kinds of rituals or artworks could help us navigate that without either romanticizing the past or surrendering fully to the vectors?

4. Tiny Sacraments in a Slop World

For all the talk of slop and stacks and metabolisms, there’s a stubborn fact that never quite dissolves:

People are still looking for something to believe in.

That doesn’t go away just because the feed is flooded or the content is synthetic. If anything, the more saturated and automated everything feels, the more intensely people start searching for islands of meaning—small moments that feel charged, intentional, significant.

That’s where the language of sacrament keeps tugging at me.

In a religious context, a sacrament is ordinary matter treated as extraordinary:
bread, wine, oil, water, ash, gesture, word. The object itself isn’t rare. The attention wrapped around it is.

We live now in a world where slop is the default matter:
endless clips, templates, filters, AI renders, auto-voiced scripts, automated playlists. It’s all cheap, repeatable, and easy to spoof.

So the question becomes:

What does a sacrament look like in a slop world?

Not in some abstract, theological sense, but in the everyday mess of Miami, group chats, scrolls, and studio work.

WhatsApp saints and glitchy miracles

I’ve seen low-res devotional images do more real emotional work than many “high art” pieces.

A JPEG of the Virgen de Guadalupe, compressed to hell, shared in a family WhatsApp.
A screenshot of a Bible verse with sparkly GIF stickers layered on top.
A TikTok where a young woman cries, talking about how God “sent” her this exact video when she needed it.
A Spanish-language AI-generated angel with slightly uncanny hands, accompanied by a caption about protection.

From a design standpoint, a lot of this is slop:
over-filtered, algorithmically boosted, uncredited, semi-plagiarized.

From the inside of a believer’s nervous system, it might be the one piece of content that lands with force that day. A tiny sacrament: not because the pixels are pure, but because the timing, mood, and intention line up.

Even non-religious corners of the feed are full of proto-sacraments:

“If you’re seeing this, it’s a sign to keep going.”

“This sound will change your life.”

“The universe wants you to hear this right now.”

It’s easy to mock that language. Sometimes I do. But it’s also a signal: the Stack is being quietly conscripted into folk metaphysics. The recommendation engine becomes fate. The “For You” page becomes a minor oracle. Viral sounds become secular hymns.

When you add AI to that mix—AI voiceovers, AI angels, AI pastors, AI tarot readers—the line between slop and sacrament gets even weirder. The same generative pipelines that churn out disposable content are being used to dress up spiritual messages, predictions, and promises.

The dead internet hasn’t killed belief. It’s just routing it through new pipes.

Storefront churches, LED signs, and the Stack

In Miami, this is not theoretical.

I walk past small storefront churches in strip plazas:
neon crosses, “JESUS TE AMA” in bold vinyl letters, services in Spanish, English, Creole, Portuguese. A few plastic chairs, a drum kit, a keyboard, maybe a small LED sign with rotating Bible verses.

These churches are often operating on thin margins, in awkward spaces between hair salons, tax offices, and vape shops. They’re hyper-local, grounded in migration, class, family histories, and neighborhood dynamics.

But their congregants also carry phones. Their sermons also get clipped. Their communities exist both in the room and in the chat. They compete for attention not just with other churches, but with the entire slop metabolism: streaming platforms, shorts, AI-generated “spiritual” content, crypto prophets, hustle pastors, algorithmic wellness.

You can imagine the Stack pressing in on these spaces at every layer:

Earth: rent, flooding, heat, traffic, voltage.

Cloud: platforms carrying their livestreams, payment processors handling tithes, AI tools writing announcements.

City: zoning, noise complaints, police, landlords, parking.

Address: WhatsApp groups, email lists, YouTube channels, usernames.

Interface: LED screens, phones, projected lyrics, chat windows.

User: the pastor, the abuela, the teenager, the lurker in the comments.

For many people, these churches are some of the last places where time feels thick instead of thin—where you’re not supposed to scroll, multitask, or split your attention. You sing, listen, pray, cry, hug. Your body is fully there.

At the same time, the Stack keeps leaking in: notifications buzzing in pockets, phones held up to capture “the good part,” AI tools quietly used in the background for graphics, subtitles, sermon notes.

The sacraments and the slop are in the same room.

That tension is one of the reasons I made Simulation Faith:
a baby Jesus statue wearing a VR headset, held in a way that’s both devotional and slightly absurd. It sits at the intersection of:

cheap devotional kitsch,

high-tech fetish objects,

and very real desires for transcendence, protection, and meaning.

It’s not making fun of belief. It’s asking:

What happens when our images of the sacred start wearing the same hardware as our games, our porn, our work, our doomscrolls?

In a city like Miami, that question won’t stay theoretical for long.

My studio as a small heretical chapel

Which brings me back to the studio.

When I look around my space at Bakehouse—servers, screens, sculptures, shopping carts, baby figures, old iPads, mockups of AI churches, sketches of roosters, notes for this book—I realize the studio itself is a kind of heretical chapel inside the Stack.

It’s not sacred in any official sense. But it is a place where I:

step slightly out of the slop flow,

collect artifacts from the metabolism (e-waste, images, interfaces),

and try to re-arrange them into scenes that invite a different kind of attention.

A Doomscrolling Treadmill is a sort of anti-sacrament:

same gestures as your daily phone ritual,

but framed, amplified, ritualized until they look absurd enough to notice.

Smart Shoppers is another:

brains where groceries should be,

a cart that carries cognition instead of goods.

Simulation Faith is a third:

a devotional object wearing a headset,

both joke and prophecy at once.

I don’t think of these works as sermons. They don’t tell you what to believe. They don’t offer escape. If anything, they lean into the discomfort of being fully inside all of this—alive, implicated, sometimes complicit, sometimes resistant.

What they can do is create tiny pockets of what I’d still call sacramental attention:

you stop,

you look longer than the algorithm expects,

you feel something—confusion, recognition, unease, empathy, maybe even tenderness,

and for a moment the default metabolism is interrupted.

That interruption is small, but it matters to me.

In a slop world, any moment where you’re fully present with a single thing—an artwork, a person, a sentence, a memory—already feels like it’s breaking a rule.

Toward techno-spiritual churches

All of this sets up where we’re going next:

If slop is the default matter,

and sacraments are rare pockets of charged attention,

and the Stack is the structure through which both now travel,

then the next question is:

What happens when we start building explicitly spiritual spaces on top of that arrangement?

Not just a baby Jesus in a VR headset as a sculpture, but:

AI pastors delivering sermons generated from prompts and analytics,

prayer bots that talk to you in DMs,

personalized scripture feeds tuned to your psychological profile,

virtual shrines accessible through headsets and phones,

physical churches wired to AI tools for music, visuals, translation, “prophecy.”

Some of that already exists in prototype form. Some of it is inevitable, given the incentives. Some of it will be beautiful. Some of it will be manipulative and predatory. Most of it will be a confusing mix of both.

As someone raised inside both faith and feeds, I’m not interested in easy answers like:

“AI religion is fake, real religion is pure,”

or “technology will save spirituality.”

I’m interested in the messy middle:

the storefront church with an LED sign and a WhatsApp prayer chain,

the abuela who sends you a JPEG of a saint that feels more sincere than any branded AI wellness app,

the future Wynwood-style techno-cathedral where a large language model writes half the liturgy.

The next chapter will dive into that space:

Simulation Faith as a key artwork,

Miami as a testbed for hybrid spirituality (Catholic, evangelical, Afro-diasporic, New Age, wellness, crypto cults, AI hype),

thinkers like Erik Davis and Yuk Hui as companions for this weird walk.

What does it mean to have AI pastors in a world where intelligence is already a rentable utility?

How do belief, ritual, and vulnerability behave when the Stack becomes the primary mediator of spiritual experience?

And how can an artist build alternate “techno-spiritual” prototypes that are honest about the power dynamics without collapsing into cynicism or naive worship of the machine?

Even in the thickest slop, people are still trying to bless things.

They bless meals, days, relationships, timelines, feeds, future selves.
They bless their hustle, their health, their family back home, their followers.
They forward prayers, like posts, share testimonies.

The dead internet is not as dead as it thinks. There are still little sparks moving through it, looking for a place to land.

My job, in the chapters ahead, is to track what happens when we start building entire architectures—both physical and digital—to catch those sparks.

And to ask, gently but persistently:

Whose god is this machine really serving?
And what kind of altars do we want to build inside it while we’re still here, still embodied, still capable of saying no, yes, I don’t know, amen, enough.
